# Scenario-Based Q&A — Regression and Regularization

**Situation**: You built a linear regression model to predict customer lifetime value. It has a great R² of 0.85 on the training set, but the finance team says the coefficient for "days since signup" flips sign every time you retrain on a new month of data. What's going on and what would you do?
**Answer**: This is a classic multicollinearity symptom — the coefficient is unstable, not necessarily wrong, because "days since signup" is likely correlated with other tenure-related features (e.g., "number of purchases," "account age bucket"). I'd compute VIF for all features, identify the correlated cluster, and either drop/combine redundant features or switch to Ridge/ElasticNet, which shrink correlated coefficients together instead of arbitrarily assigning weight to one. I'd also check coefficient stability via bootstrap resampling before trusting any single coefficient for a business narrative.

**Situation**: Your marketing team has 200 spend channels and wants a model that tells them exactly which channels to cut. Plain linear regression gives wildly different coefficients each time you add a new week of data.
**Answer**: With p (channels) large and many channels correlated, OLS is underdetermined/unstable. I'd use Lasso or ElasticNet — Lasso will zero out redundant channels for a clean "keep vs. cut" list, but if channels are highly correlated I'd prefer ElasticNet since Lasso arbitrarily picks one of a correlated group and that pick can flip between refits. I would also validate the selected channel set via cross-validation and bootstrap stability before letting the finance team make budget decisions based on a single fit.

**Situation**: A model built on 15 years of housing data has a residual-vs-fitted plot that fans out clearly (small errors on cheap houses, huge errors on expensive ones).
**Answer**: This is heteroscedasticity, violating the constant-variance OLS assumption. Point estimates (coefficients) stay roughly unbiased, but standard errors and confidence intervals are wrong, which matters if anyone downstream uses them for hypothesis testing or risk bounds. Fixes: log-transform the target (common for price/money variables), use weighted least squares, or report robust (heteroscedasticity-consistent / "sandwich") standard errors. I'd re-plot residuals after the fix to confirm the funnel shape disappears.

**Situation**: You add 50 more engineered features to a regression model and R² goes from 0.62 to 0.71. A stakeholder says "great, ship it." What do you check first?
**Answer**: R² never decreases when you add features, even irrelevant ones — so a rise alone doesn't prove the new features are useful. I'd check Adjusted R² and, more importantly, cross-validated / held-out error (RMSE on a test set the model never saw). If held-out performance didn't meaningfully improve (or got worse), the extra features are just fitting noise, and I'd trim them via Lasso or a feature-selection pass rather than shipping a bloated, overfit model.

**Situation**: You're asked to predict ad revenue vs. spend, and you know the relationship has diminishing returns (saturation) at high spend levels. A junior teammate proposes adding degree-8 polynomial terms to "capture the curve better."
**Answer**: Degree-8 polynomials will fit the training curve almost perfectly but oscillate wildly and extrapolate nonsensically outside the observed spend range — a classic overfitting trap, especially dangerous for a business model used to simulate "what if we doubled spend." I'd start with a degree-2 polynomial (or a domain-appropriate saturating function like log or a Hill/logistic curve), pair it with Ridge regularization, and validate on a held-out spend range to make sure extrapolation behaves sensibly, not just interpolation within the training range.

**Situation**: Legal/compliance asks: "Can you explain, in plain terms, why the loan-approval regression model weighted `zip_code`-derived features so heavily, and whether that's proxying for a protected attribute?"
**Answer**: I'd first check whether zip-code-derived features are highly correlated with income, race, or other protected/proxy variables (VIF and correlation analysis), since an unregularized model can inflate a proxy variable's coefficient purely due to collinearity artifacts rather than genuine causal signal. I'd re-fit with Ridge/Lasso to see if the coefficient shrinks or drops, run a fairness audit (disparate impact by group), and if the proxy signal persists and is not defensible, remove or transform that feature — a regression coefficient is not proof of a real business driver, and compliance needs the distinction explained clearly.

**Situation**: Your regression model's assumptions all check out on synthetic test data, but in production, predictions are systematically biased upward for a new customer segment that didn't exist in training data.
**Answer**: This is a distribution-shift / extrapolation problem, not a violation of OLS assumptions per se — the model is being asked to predict outside the support of its training data. I'd add monitoring that flags when incoming feature values fall outside training ranges, retrain regularly as new segments accumulate data, and consider segment-specific models or interaction terms if the new segment has a structurally different relationship between features and target rather than just a shifted average.
